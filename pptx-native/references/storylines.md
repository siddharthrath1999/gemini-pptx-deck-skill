# Storylines

A deck is an argument or an explanation with a beginning, a middle and an end. Decide which before choosing any layout.

## First decisions

- **Purpose.** What should the audience know, decide or be able to do afterwards? Write it in one sentence. That sentence, or a version of it, belongs near the start and at the end.
- **Audience.** What do they already know, what do they care about, and how much time do they have? Executives want the answer first. Learners need the question first.
- **Use.** Presented live (less text, more notes) or read alone (slides must stand on their own)? If unknown, write slides that can be read alone and put delivery cues in the notes.
- **Length.** If the user gave a number, hit it within about ten percent. If not, fit the request: a pitch is 10 to 15 slides, an executive update 8 to 12, a benchmark or analysis 20 to 35, a learning deck 30 to 50, a teardown or deep report 30 to 60. Go well past the range (60 to 150) when the brief says detailed, comprehensive, deep or full, or covers many entities or dimensions. Go to the bottom of the range when it asks for a summary or a quick deck. Say the planned count in your assumptions line; do not ask.
- **Earning the length.** A long deck needs more research, not more words. For a benchmark of six companies on five dimensions, that means a profile section per company, a comparison section per dimension, worked examples, and an appendix of full tables. If the material for a slide is thin, research more or cut the slide.

## The outline test

Write one headline per slide before any detail. Then read only the headlines, top to bottom.

- Do they tell the whole story without the bodies?
- Does each one say something the previous one did not?
- Could a reader stop a third of the way in and still have the main point?

Rework until yes. Most weak decks fail here, not in the design.

## Patterns by deck type

Layout names in brackets are suggestions.

### Executive or board update
Answer first.
1. Cover.
2. The headline message and the decision needed (statement, or kpis).
3. Three to five supporting points, one slide each, strongest first (chart, table, comparison).
4. Risks and what would change the view (cards, matrix).
5. Options and recommendation (comparison, pros_cons).
6. Next steps with owners and dates (next_steps).
7. Appendix: detail, method, sources.

### Strategy or recommendation
Situation, complication, question, answer.
1. Where things stand (kpis, chart). 2. What changed or is at stake (statement, big_number). 3. The question this deck answers (statement). 4. The answer in one slide (statement or cards). 5. Evidence for each part of the answer. 6. Alternatives considered and why not (comparison). 7. Plan, costs, risks (roadmap, table, matrix). 8. What is needed from the audience (next_steps).

### Investor or sales pitch
1. Cover with a one-line description. 2. The problem, with one concrete example or number (big_number, statement). 3. Why now (timeline, chart). 4. The product or offer (cards, process). 5. How it works or what is different (before_after, comparison). 6. Proof: traction, results, customers (kpis, chart, quote). 7. Market and model (chart, split_bar, funnel). 8. Competition (scorecard, matrix). 9. Team or delivery plan (cards, roadmap). 10. The ask (statement, next_steps).
One idea per slide, little text, large numbers.

### Benchmark or competitive analysis
1. Cover, scope and method (who was compared, on what, from which sources, as of when). 2. The headline finding (statement). 3. The comparison at a glance (scorecard or heatmap). 4. One section per dimension: a divider, then the ranking (bar_list, chart), then what explains it (cards, comparison). 5. One profile slide per entity for long decks (kpis or comparison with the same anatomy each time). 6. Patterns across the set (matrix, two_column). 7. Implications and recommendations (cards, next_steps). 8. Appendix: full tables, definitions, sources.
Keep entity order and colours identical on every slide.

### Review (quarter, project, campaign, creative work)
1. What was planned and what happened (kpis, table). 2. What went well, with evidence (chart, quote, cards). 3. What did not, with causes (waterfall chart, before_after, bullets). 4. What was learned (recap). 5. What changes next (roadmap, next_steps).
For creative reviews: show the work's goals, each piece against them, the response data, then what to keep, change and drop.

### Learning or teaching deck
Question before answer, and one concept at a time.
1. Cover and agenda with a legend for how to read the slides. 2. Why this matters, through a concrete case. 3. For each concept: a question for the room (question), the answer with evidence (chart, split_bar, comparison), a worked example (process, before_after), common mistakes (pros_cons, cards). 4. A recap at the end of each part (recap). 5. A final map or cheat sheet, what to try next (cards, next_steps). 6. Appendix: glossary, method, sources.
Carry one running example or character through the whole deck.
When the brief says fun, simple, beginner or for everyone: keep each slide to about 40 words on screen, use larger type and fewer items (three cards, not five), and move technical detail (codes, message names, exact timings) into the speaker notes or an appendix. Depth stays in the deck; the slide shows only the part people need to see. Use tone to mark slide roles (for example accent for questions, light for answers, dark for the mechanics).

### Walkthrough or runbook
1. What this covers, when to use it, who it is for (cover, cards). 2. Prerequisites and access (table, bullets). 3. The overall flow on one slide (process). 4. Each stage: numbered steps with owner, time and command (steps, code), then how to verify (cards, table). 5. What can go wrong and what to do (table, matrix). 6. Escalation and contacts (next_steps, table). 7. Appendix: reference values, links.
Number steps continuously across slides with `start`.

### Research or analytical report
1. The question and why it matters. 2. Summary of findings on one slide (recap or cards). 3. Method and data in brief. 4. Findings, one per slide, each with its chart and a takeaway. 5. Limits and what is uncertain. 6. Implications. 7. Appendix with full method, tables and sources.

### Mixed or unusual requests
Combine patterns. A product launch deck might open like a pitch, teach like a learning deck in the middle and close like an executive update. Choose by what each part of the audience needs at that moment.

## Devices that carry long decks

- **Part structure.** Three to five parts, each opened by a `section` slide and closed by a `recap`.
- **Signposting.** Use the kicker for "Part 2 · Revenue" so every slide says where it sits.
- **Question and answer pairs.** A question slide, then a slide whose headline is the answer. Good every eight to twelve slides in a live session.
- **A running example.** One customer, order, project or dataset followed from start to end.
- **Repeated anatomy.** When covering several entities, use the same layout for each so differences stand out.
- **A closing map.** One slide that places everything covered into a single picture or table.
- **Appendix.** Method, definitions, assumptions, full tables and numbered sources. Refer to it from the footer ("Note 5") instead of crowding the slide.

## Depth

A deeper deck has more evidence, more comparison, more worked examples and more precise numbers. It does not have longer sentences. For each main point ask:

- What is the number, and against what baseline or peer?
- What explains it? What is the mechanism?
- What is the counter-example or caveat?
- What does it look like in one concrete case?
- So what, for this audience?

Each answer that matters becomes a slide. Each that does not, goes in notes or is cut.

## Research standards

- Search before you write. Prefer primary sources: company filings, shareholder letters, regulators, statistical agencies, the organisation's own documents, peer-reviewed work. Next best: established research firms, brokerages and major news outlets.
- Treat SEO blogs, marketing agencies, data resellers and content farms as leads, not sources. Use one for a headline figure only when nothing better exists, label the figure "est." and name the source on the slide. Prefer two independent sources for any number the argument rests on.
- Record source and date for every figure as you collect it, and carry it to the slide's `source` line and the Sources slide.
- Use the most recent data available and state its date. Do not mix periods in one comparison without saying so.
- When you estimate, say so, show the arithmetic in notes or the appendix, and label the slide "estimate".
- When you cannot find something, write a bracketed placeholder and report it. Never fill a gap with a plausible number.
